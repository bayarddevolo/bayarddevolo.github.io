Cuba

My Cuba research spans two interconnected projects: a historical study of women's role in the 1950s insurrection that brought Castro to power, and an ongoing investigation of Cuba's active—and largely overlooked—agency in the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis. Drawing on extensive archival research in Cuban and international archives, ethnographic fieldwork, and interviews with political actors, this work examines how gender, revolutionary identity, and national martyrdom have shaped Cuban politics and international conflict from the insurrection to the Cold War.

Current Research Project

My current work repositions Cuba as an active agent—rather than a mere backdrop or pawn—in the Cuban Missile Crisis. Drawing from Cuban, Soviet, American, and other international archives, I examine how concepts of masculinity, national martyrdom, and revolutionary identity shaped Cuba's diplomatic posture and decision-making during this near-nuclear confrontation. This project challenges conventional two-superpower narratives and restores Cuban voices and perspectives to the historical record.

Women and the Cuban Insurrection: How Gender Shaped Castro's Victory

Cambridge University Press, 2018

In Women and the Cuban Insurrection, I show how women were essential to the 26th of July Movement's success—not only as combatants but as organizers, fundraisers, smugglers, and couriers operating in both urban and rural networks. Rather than treating women's participation as a footnote to the male guerrilla narrative, the book foregrounds gender as a constitutive dimension of the insurrection itself. Through extensive archival research and oral histories with surviving participants, I demonstrate that gender ideology both constrained and enabled women's political action, as the movement simultaneously relied on and downplayed women's contributions. The book also examines how the post-revolutionary state subsequently managed and memorialized women's roles, shaping what could be remembered and claimed.
